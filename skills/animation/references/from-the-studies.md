# What finished videos showed about animation

These findings come from studying four finished long-form explainer videos frame by frame, then checking them across about forty more (shots, retention exports, breakdown sheets, stems), with the editor's answers. They add to the motion rules; they don't replace them. Numbers describe those videos and are starting points, not targets.

## Where animation goes

- **The script decides how much.** There's no rule on too much or too little. The breakdown plans animation for about 16–30% of the words; a long AI-made story or illustrated history can add more.
- **Animation takes a line only when the line has a structure:** a mechanism, a number, a dated event, a route, a new term or a source. Never for decoration.
- **Chains of animation with no presenter between them are fine** when the script needs them (one to two minutes at most in the explained parts).
- **The device for what can't be filmed is decided early,** and AI-made stills and films are now a regular one: an AI-made story, illustration, collage or maps for things nobody can shoot, such as what happens inside the body, a city a century ago, or a long stretch of history.
- **A long story block works.** An eight-minute AI-made story with no presenter held viewers about as well as the explained parts of the same video. Plan it as one block in the breakdown.

## How it looks

- **One graphic world per video** (it held across all forty videos, older ones included), built from references around the topic (how its own experts draw it), sometimes from time constraints: for example the plans and models a field's own experts draw, the maps its forecasts use, old printed plates for an old science, or archive and paper for history.
- **One or two accent colours that mean something in the topic.** The rest stays restrained.
- **Chapter cards are built inside the video's graphic world** (a label on the animated scene, a card on the map, a parchment plate), and mostly stay in the video's style. The chapters themselves are already set in the script.
- **The title card is a fixed format:** "<channel> presents" and a wordplay title, about 8% into the video (2–15% across about forty videos), named by the writer. Every video gets one.
- **Abstract things are made concrete in the same world:** cost as coins stacking on a tower, a chapter name as a sign on the model.
- **One template per kind of moment,** such as a definition card for each new term, used once per term.
- **The same template or shot returns only as a callback** to a specific earlier point. Never repeat a shot, animation or image otherwise.

## How it moves and enters

- **Every animation enters and leaves on a hard cut.** No wipes, dissolves or exit animations; the first frame is never empty.
- **Animations built to the voiceover keep their length** to within a frame or two in the final edit, so time them to the VO from the start.

## History and procedure

- **Tell history through a person where possible.** The person can be a real historical figure (timeline points tied to a real person) or a made-up character. If the person is real, every event tied to them must be factually correct.
- **Step-by-step procedure animations lose viewers.** Rule-by-rule diagrams, strings of capacity figures and policy timelines were the stretches that held worst. Make them more engaging, keep them short, or carry them through a person.

## Sound with animation

- **Every element that arrives gets a sound** chosen to the object (coins for money, paper for documents, a ratchet for something counting up, a horn or engine for travel). This is firm.
- **Title and chapter cards get a hit or a riser.**
- **The heaviest sound sits on the main explanation graphic or the story's peak.**
- **How heavy the low end is follows the graphic world:** a construction-like world or a trip takes heavy low booms; a paper world stays light.
